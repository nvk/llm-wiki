# Anterior Dislocation

**Domain:** [[emergency/_index|Emergency Radiology]] ([Emergency Radiology](_index.md))  
**Source:** Emergency Radiology: The Requisites (Pages 132–133)  

## Summary & Core Concepts
Anterior shoulder dislocation. Hill-Sachs fracture (arrow). Bankart fracture (arrowhead).

## Key Radiological & Clinical Findings
- **Radiography (Plain Film):** Essential baseline imaging for Anterior Dislocation; evaluates anatomical contours, alignment, lucencies, and gross calcifications.
- **Computed Tomography (CT):** Gold-standard modality for multiplanar delineation, density characterization, and staging of Anterior Dislocation.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** High soft-tissue resolution with exquisite contrast resolution for soft tissues, marrow space, and fluid kinetics.
- **Ultrasound / Doppler:** Real-time, non-ionizing dynamic assessment with color and spectral Doppler evaluation of vascular flow.

## Differential Diagnosis & High-Yield Pitfalls
- Diagnostic mimics sharing radiographic or cross-sectional morphology with Anterior Dislocation.
- Key discriminator: Contrast enhancement patterns, tissue composition (fat, calcium, blood products), and anatomical distribution.
- Clinical correlation: Integration with patient presentation, laboratory biomarkers, and acuity dictates definitive diagnostic pathway.

Posterior Dislocation
Posterior dislocations of the glenohumeral joint (Fig. 4-5)
usually occur with violent muscle contractions, as seen with
seizures, or electric shocks. The dislocation can be either
subacromial, subglenoid, or subspinous. It is an ­uncommon
injury that frequently coexists with an impacted fracture
of the anterior humeral head due to trauma to the humeral
head on the glenoid fossa during dislocation. It is difficult to
detect clinically, especially in obese or muscular individuals.
On frontal radiographs, posterior dislocations may go unrec­
ognized in up to 50% of cases. Multiple signs have been
described as helpful for detection of this subtle injury on
frontal projections. The “positive rim” sign is an increase
in the distance between the articular cortex of the humeral
head and the anterior glenoid rim to more than 6 mm. The
“light bulb” sign is a humeral headfixed in internal rotation,
resembling a light bulb. The “trough line” is a curvilinear
dense line parallel to the articular margin of the humeral
head, representing an impacted fracture of the anterior as­
pect of the humeral head that coexists with the dislocation.
