# Spontaneous Cervical Dissection

**Domain:** [[emergency/_index|Emergency Radiology]] ([Emergency Radiology](_index.md))  
**Source:** Emergency Radiology: The Requisites (Pages 38–40)  

## Summary & Core Concepts
Arterial dissection secondary to blunt trauma. Internal jugular vein obscures internal carotid artery. V4) are the most common sites of intracranial dissection.

## Key Radiological & Clinical Findings
- **Radiography (Plain Film):** Essential baseline imaging for Spontaneous Cervical Dissection; evaluates anatomical contours, alignment, lucencies, and gross calcifications.
- **Computed Tomography (CT):** rounded by a thin rim of contrast enhancement. Internal carotid artery dissection—CT and MR angiograms.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** High soft-tissue resolution with exquisite contrast resolution for soft tissues, marrow space, and fluid kinetics.
- **Ultrasound / Doppler:** Real-time, non-ionizing dynamic assessment with color and spectral Doppler evaluation of vascular flow.

## Differential Diagnosis & High-Yield Pitfalls
- Diagnostic mimics sharing radiographic or cross-sectional morphology with Spontaneous Cervical Dissection.
- Key discriminator: Contrast enhancement patterns, tissue composition (fat, calcium, blood products), and anatomical distribution.
- Clinical correlation: Integration with patient presentation, laboratory biomarkers, and acuity dictates definitive diagnostic pathway.

a thickened wall and a narrowed eccentric lumen sur­

CT angiography has also become the initial method for

the neck, face, and head based on availability, efficiency,
and similar profiles of sensitivity and specificity as in blunt
trauma. The high sensitivity of CT for the detection of
small amounts of contrast extravasation or air in the soft
tissues helps to localize subtle injuries to major vessels that
might otherwise go unnoticed. An understanding of the
expected trajectory of bullets or knives based on skin en­

shrapnel often cause substantial artifacts that limit the di­
agnostic value of CTA. Digital subtraction angiography can
usually overcome this limitation through the use of mul­
tiple oblique projections.
Spontaneous Intracranial Dissection
Intracranial arteries lack an external elastic lamina and have
thinner media and adventitia compared with extracranial
vessels. Therefore, intracranial dissections may ­ behave
differently. If the dissection occurs between the intima
and media, then luminal stenosis or occlusion may lead to
ischemia or infarct similar to the extracranial setting. If the
dissection plane is between the media and adventitia, then
(dissecting) pseudoaneurysm formation is likely. Due to
the thin media and adventitia, rupture and subarachnoid
hemorrhage may occur. SAH is reported in approximately
