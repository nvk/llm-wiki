# Other Radiographic Signs of Disease

**Domain:** [[cardio/_index|Cardiothoracic Radiology]] ([Cardiothoracic Radiology](_index.md))  
**Source:** Thoracic Radiology: The Requisites (Pages 66–68)

## Key Radiological & Clinical Findings
- **Radiography (Plain Film):** Essential baseline imaging for Other Radiographic Signs of Disease; evaluates anatomical contours, alignment, lucencies, and gross calcifications.
- **Computed Tomography (CT):** Gold-standard modality for multiplanar delineation, density characterization, and staging of Other Radiographic Signs of Disease.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** High soft-tissue resolution with exquisite contrast resolution for soft tissues, marrow space, and fluid kinetics.
- **Ultrasound / Doppler:** Real-time, non-ionizing dynamic assessment with color and spectral Doppler evaluation of vascular flow.

## Differential Diagnosis & High-Yield Pitfalls
- Diagnostic mimics sharing radiographic or cross-sectional morphology with Other Radiographic Signs of Disease.
- Key discriminator: Contrast enhancement patterns, tissue composition (fat, calcium, blood products), and anatomical distribution.
- Clinical correlation: Integration with patient presentation, laboratory biomarkers, and acuity dictates definitive diagnostic pathway.

Other Radiographic Signs of Disease
Localization of Disease
The anatomic localization of disease is usually easily
determined on standard posteroanterior and lateral chest
radiographs. A classic radiographic sign that helps in the
localization of disease processes that have caused an
increase in opacification is the silhouette sign (see Figs.
1-38 and 1-39). Normally, the mediastinal and diaphrag­
matic contours are visible because of their inherent con­
trast with the adjacent air-containing lung. When a lesion
or opacity is situated in a portion of the lung adjacent to
a mediastinal or diaphragmatic border, the border can no
longer be seen. This sign is apparent only when structures
have been adequately penetrated.

Distribution of Disease within the Lung
The distribution of disease in the lungs may be a clue to
the diagnosis. Gravity is often an important factor. For
example, aspiration pneumonia typically occurs in the
­posterior segments of the upper lobes and superior seg­
ments of the lower lobes when the patient is supine, and
when the patient is erect, the basal segments of the lower
lobes are involved.
Because there is much more bloodflow to the base of
the lungs than the apices in the erect position, certain pro­
cesses related to the pulmonary circulation occur at the
bases. For example, pulmonary infarction is much more
common in the lower lobes, and metastatic lesions tend to
occur at the bases.
