## Key Radiological & Clinical Findings
- **Radiography (Plain Film):** third (arrows) is shown in the anteroposterior radiograph of the clavicle.
- **Computed Tomography (CT):** Gold-standard modality for multiplanar delineation, density characterization, and staging of Fractures of the Scapula.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** High soft-tissue resolution with exquisite contrast resolution for soft tissues, marrow space, and fluid kinetics.
- **Ultrasound / Doppler:** Real-time, non-ionizing dynamic assessment with color and spectral Doppler evaluation of vascular flow.

## Differential Diagnosis & High-Yield Pitfalls
- Diagnostic mimics sharing radiographic or cross-sectional morphology with Fractures of the Scapula.
- Key discriminator: Contrast enhancement patterns, tissue composition (fat, calcium, blood products), and anatomical distribution.
- Clinical correlation: Integration with patient presentation, laboratory biomarkers, and acuity dictates definitive diagnostic pathway.

­Approximately 80% of fractures of the scapula involve

fractures have the potential to threaten the function of

can contribute to instability of the glenohumeral joint, ar­

bundle injury. Early recognition may lead to a change in
the type of therapy provided to polytrauma patients.
Fractures of the Clavicle
The primary mechanism causing fractures of the clavicle
(Box 4-5) is a fall onto the superolateral shoulder. Because
of the strength of the sternoclavicular ligament, the force
exits the clavicle in the midshaft, resulting in the most
common pattern of clavicle fracture: fracture of the mid­
dle third. The most widely used classification system (by
Allman) divides clavicular fractures into three groups by
location: group 1, middle third; group 2, lateral third; and
group 3, medial third. Group 2 (or lateral third) fractures are
further divided into three distinct types by Neer, depend­
ing on whether the coracoclavicular ligament is involved.
The middle third of the clavicle (group 1) (Fig. 4-2A) is
involved in 65% to 85% of all fractures. Complete fractures
typically result in superior displacement of the medial
fragment (due to pull of the sternocleidomastoid muscle)
and inferior displacement of the lateral fragment (due to
gravity pull by the shoulder joint). Overriding of frag­
