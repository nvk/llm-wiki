# Bronchogenic Cyst

**Domain:** [[cardio/_index|Cardiothoracic Radiology]] ([Cardiothoracic Radiology](_index.md))  
**Source:** Thoracic Radiology: The Requisites (Pages 82–85)  

## Summary & Core Concepts

## Key Radiological & Clinical Findings
- **Radiography (Plain Film):** can be documented by an expiration radiograph or CT.
- **Computed Tomography (CT):** On CT images of the chest (Fig. C, Contrast-enhanced CT confirms the vascular supply.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** may have high signal intensity on T1-weighted images.
- **Ultrasound / Doppler:** Real-time, non-ionizing dynamic assessment with color and spectral Doppler evaluation of vascular flow.

## Differential Diagnosis & High-Yield Pitfalls
- Diagnostic mimics sharing radiographic or cross-sectional morphology with Bronchogenic Cyst.
- Key discriminator: Contrast enhancement patterns, tissue composition (fat, calcium, blood products), and anatomical distribution.
- Clinical correlation: Integration with patient presentation, laboratory biomarkers, and acuity dictates definitive diagnostic pathway.

PULMONARY VESSELS
Anomalies of the Pulmonary Arteries
Absence of the Main Pulmonary Artery and
Proximal Interruption
Absence of the main pulmonary artery frequently consists
of atresia of the artery’s proximal portion or of its entire
length. The right and left main pulmonary arteries persist
and connect to the aorta by a ductus.
Congenital unilateral absence of a pulmonary artery
usually occurs with cardiac lesions, but it may be an iso­
latedfinding when it occurs on the right side. Chest
radiographicfindings include evidence of a small lung
as manifested by cardiac and mediastinal shift, absence
of the pulmonary arterial shadow, elevation of the hemi­
diaphragm, and decrease in pulmonary vessels in the
affected lung with oligemia (Fig. 2-20). There may be
hyperinflation and ­herniation of the opposite lung across
the midline. This entity must be distinguished from
Swyer-James syndrome, which shares many of the radio­
graphic features. This syndrome results from bronchi­
olitis and is always associated with air trapping, which
can be documented by an expiration radiograph or CT.
Absence of the pulmonary artery can be confirmed with
CT scanning (see Fig. 2-20), which also reveals the small
ipsilateral hemithorax and the enlarged intercostal vessels
and transpleural collaterals that supply the affected lung.
Although CT is the preferred method, MRI with either
spin-echo or gradient-echo images also shows absence of
the right pulmonary artery.
