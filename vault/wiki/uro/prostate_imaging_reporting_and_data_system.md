European Society of Urogenital Radiology (ESUR) published guidelines 
for the minimal requirements to acquire mp-MRI and a scoring system to 
assess the risk of the presence of clinically significant PCa. This Prostate 
Imaging—Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) was updated in 2015 in 
a joint collaboration with the ESUR, the American College of Radiology 
(ACR) and the AdMeTech Foundation. This PI-RADS v2 addressed the 
limitations of the first version and enabled global standardisation.
Prostate imaging can be performed on equipment with a magnetic 
field strength of both 1.5 tesla (1.5 T) and 3 tesla (3 T), but preferably 
at 3 T. Compared with 1.5 T, 3 T is more sensitive to susceptibility and 
signal heterogeneity, which can cause artefacts. However, 3 T also allows 
for an increased signal-to-noise ratio and higher spatial and temporal 
resolution, which can outweigh these concerns. The acquisition protocol 
should include T2 weighted, DWI and DCE.
An endorectal coil that is integrated with external phased-array coils 
can improve signal-to-noise ratio but may increase cost and time of 
the examination, deform the prostate, cause artefacts and may be 
uncomfortable for patients. Because high diagnostic quality imaging 
has been obtained at both 1.5 T and 3 T without the use of an endorectal 
coil, it is recommended to strive for optimisation of the imaging protocols, 
instead of using an endorectal coil.
PI-RADS v2 is used to assess, on a 5-point scale, the likelihood that 
mp-MRI findings correlate with the presence of a clinically significant 
PCa for each lesion in the prostate gland (Table 32.4), ranging from a 
very low (PI-RADS 1) to a very high (PI-RADS 5) likelihood. In the 
PI-RADS v2 recommendations, a clinically significant PCa is defined 
as a Gleason score of 7 or higher (also 3 + 4), and/or volume ≥ 0.5 cc 
and/or extraprostatic extension. The PI-RADS v2 categories do not 
include management recommendations, because these also depend on 
other laboratory and clinical factors (e.g. PSA level, clinical history and 
local preferences).
PI-RADS assessment in the peripheral zone is different from the 
assessment in the transition zone. In the peripheral zone, DWI is the 
primary determining sequence, while in the transition zone, T2 weighted 
is dominant (Fig. 32.3). When the quality of T2 weighted and DWI is 
good, DCE plays a minor role in determining the PI-RADS category. DCE 
does not influence the scoring when the finding has a low (PI-RADS 1 
or 2) or high (PI-RADS 4 or 5) likelihood of clinically significant PCa. 
When a lesion in the peripheral zone is scored as PI-RADS 3 on DWI, a 
positive DCE may increase the likelihood of a clinically significant PCa 
and upgrades the assessment category to PI-RADS 4. Similarly, when 
in the transition zone, a lesion is scored as PI-RADS 3 on T2 weighted, 
DWI can upgrade the category to PI-RADS 4.

DETECTION AND LOCALISATION
There have been many changes in the way healthcare providers screen, 
diagnose and treat PCa over the past few decades. MRI of the prostate 
has become more commonly utilised for numerous purposes in the 
disease pathway. The development of a multiparametric MRI (mp-MRI) 
protocol, which comprises additional functional techniques, such as 
dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) MRI and diffusion-weighted imaging 
(DWI), next to conventional anatomical T2 weighted imaging, has made 
MRI an even more powerful imaging technique. Each sequence within 
the mp-MRI protocol provides different, but complementary, information 
to optimise PCa diagnosis, localisation and local staging and assists in 
risk stratification, biopsy guidance and treatment selection.

T2 Weighted Magnetic Resonance Imaging
In general, high-resolution T2 weighted is used to depict the zonal 
anatomy of the prostate and its surrounding structures such as the 
seminal vesicles, bladder and rectal wall, and to search for extracapsular 
extension, and invasion of the seminal vesicles. On T2 weighted images, 
areas with lower signal intensities have been correlated with higher 
Gleason grades.
Peripheral Zone
The normal, healthy peripheral zone (PI-RADS 1) has a homogeneous 
intermediate to high signal intensity and is clearly distinguishable from 
the transition and central zones (Fig. 32.5). It is usually surrounded by 
a thin T2 hypointense rim, which represents the anatomical capsule 
and is an important landmark in tumour staging.
Non-malignant conditions, such as (post-biopsy) haematomas, 
(granulomatous) prostatitis, scar tissue, atrophy, post-radiation changes 
and hormonal therapy effects, manifest as T2-hypointense areas in the 
peripheral zone. Linear, wedge-shaped lesions with mild diffuse signal 
intensity and/or indistinct margin are highly likely to be benign and 
are assigned a PI-RADS category 2 (see Fig. 32.5). A PI-RADS 3 category 
should be given to a lesion with heterogeneous signal intensity or 
non-circumscribed, rounded, moderate hypointensity (see Fig. 32.5).
In T2 weighted, PCa can appear as a focal area of low signal intensity. 
High-grade tumours, with a Gleason grade 4 or 5, usually present with 
a lower signal intensity. Low-grade tumours, with a Gleason grade 2 
or 3, can present as T2-isointense areas, or even as non-focal mildly 
hypointense abnormalities. A circumscribed, homogeneous, moderately 
hypointense area confined to the prostate and <1.5 cm in dimension is 
scored as PI-RADS 4 (see Fig. 32.5). A typical PI-RADS 5 lesion (see Fig. 
32.5) is described as a PI-RADS 4 lesion, but at least 1.5 cm in greatest 
dimension or with definite extraprostatic extension or invasive behaviour.

From an imaging standpoint, there are two components to the prostate that can be
resolved on MRI: The peripheral zone and the central gland. The central gland refers to 
both the central zone and the transition zone, as they cannot be distinguished on MRI.
• 
In younger men, the central gland is composed mostly of the central zone; however, the
transition zone enlarges as benign prostatic hyperplasia develops. These changes result in
the central gland becoming predominantly composed of transition zone in older males.
Prostate cancer
• 
MRI is able to clearly delineate the prostatic zonal anatomy (central gland versus peripheral
zone) with T2-weighted sequences. Imaging is enhanced with an endorectal coil. 
•
MRI is inappropriate for screening due to cost and low sensitivity and specificity. 
• The typical MRI appearance of prostate cancer is a T2 hypointense region within the 
T2 hyperintense peripheral zone.
• MRI may not detect all prostate cancer: Some cancer is not hypointense on T2-
weighted images, central zone cancers are difficult to detect on T2-weighted images, 
and cancer conspicuity is decreased if the peripheral zone is not T2 hyperintense.
•
MRI is also not specific: In addition to prostate cancer, the differential diagnosis of 
a region of peripheral zone T2 hypointensity includes prostatitis, hemorrhage, and
involutional changes from androgen-deprivation therapy. Advanced MRI techniques,
such as MRI spectroscopy, dynamic contrast-enhanced imaging, and diffusion imaging
may increase specificity.
• MRI spectroscopy of prostate cancer may show elevated choline and depressed citrate 
peaks compared to normal prostate.
• Dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI typically shows prostate cancer to have early 
enhancement relative to the peripheral zone.
•
Prostate cancer typically shows restricted diffusion.
