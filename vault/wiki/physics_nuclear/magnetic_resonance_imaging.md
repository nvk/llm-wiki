SECTION C  The Musculoskeletal System
MAGNETIC RESONANCE IMAGING
Routine clinical MR in the musculoskeletal system depends on a limited 
number of standard fast-spin echo and gradient-echo sequences. These 
are selected to best differentiate musculoskeletal tissues. The gains made 
in recent years have been in the form of increased spatial resolution, 
affording better anatomical detail, by using higher field strength magnets 
and improved coil design. As a result, we are beginning to see invasive 
routine MR arthrography replaced by conventional MRI. A handful of 
other sequences such as diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), motion 
reduction MR, ultrashort echo time (TE) (Fig. 38.7), and cartilage 
mapping are gaining traction, whereas dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI 
(DCE-MRI) has not yet been found to be useful.
There are no standard protocols for the MR imaging of joints but 
there are widely agreed principles. Proton density (PD) and T2 weighted 
(TW2) sequences are favoured for assessing fibrocartilage, such as the 
menisci, and ligaments and tendons. PD sequences, which often are 
fat-saturated, are preferred for hyaline cartilage. T1 weighted (TW1) and 
fat-saturated T2 weighted sequences are used for bone marrow. Each 
joint should be imaged in all three planes using a combination of these.
There are further modifications to these broad principles that can be 
helpful. Fat-saturated T2 weighted images can be optimised by not using 
very long echo times. A TE of 60 to 70 ms is long enough to ensure that 
bone marrow oedema is visible but generates much more signal-to-noise 
than a TE of 120 ms. Intermediate weighted MR—halfway between a 
PD and a T2 weighted—with a TE of about 35 ms can be useful for 
separating hyaline cartilage, fibrocartilage and synovial fluid. MR imaging

COMPUTED TOMOGRAPHY
The most common indications for CT in the musculoskeletal system 
are as extensions to conventional radiography of fractures and follow-up 
of orthopaedic implants, where three-dimensional complexity cannot 
be adequately presented on a planar image. CT also has a valuable role 
in guiding intervention.
Dual-Energy Computed Tomography
The diagnosis of crystal arthropathies in the appendicular skeleton 
can be assisted with dual-energy CT (DECT). DECT can be used 
to differentiate calcium salts by their relative attenuation of x-rays 
produced at two different peak voltages, typically 80 and 140 kVp 
(peak kilovoltage). This is achieved on modern CT machines by rapid 
alternating ‘kV switching,’ between the two voltages, on a single-source 
and single-detector system. This can be done in a single acquisition, 
ensuring that the two data sets are perfectly co-registered. Originally 
developed to identify the composition of renal stones, its use has 
been extended to the detection of soft-tissue urate deposition in gout, 
differentiating uric acid crystals from other causes of soft-tissue calcifica-
tion which otherwise look identical on plain radiography or routine 
diagnostic CT. This can be particularly useful when serum urate levels 
are normal in the acute phase of the disease, and when joint aspira-
tion is ‘dry’ and it is too early for other radiographic features to have 
appeared.
Metal Artefact Reduction
DECT can also be used to reduce beam-hardening artefact around 
orthopaedic prostheses, although there are a number of techniques 
employed to this end. Metal implants cause disproportionate attenuation 
of x-ray photons at the lower end of the spectrum. The filtered back-
projection technique used to reconstruct the CT image from the detector 
data assumes a relatively even attenuation at all energies and this results 
in the beam-hardening artefact. This effect can be ameliorated by 
increasing the kVp. This reduces the proportion of lower-energy photons 
being attenuated but it comes at a cost of increased dose of radiation 
to the patient. Monoenergetic reconstruction of DECT data can be 
performed to create images that are derived from a single virtual kVp 
(Fig. 38.6). While increasing this monoenergetic kVp does reduce 
beam-hardening artefact, it comes at a cost of an increase in noise.
Noise around metal implants results from photon starvation because 
a large proportion of the x-ray beam is attenuated. This can be addressed 
by decreasing the pitch settings, narrowing the collimation and increasing 
mA. Again, this comes with an increased radiation burden. The effect 
of the noise can be minimised during post-processing and MPR by 
increasing the reconstructed slice thickness or using slice averaging 
techniques.
There are a number of vendor-specific products that are marketed 
for metal artefact reduction in CT. Some of these use iterative reconstruc-
tion techniques instead of back-projection. Others using mask or 
segmentation techniques to model and correct for the metal object, or 
a combination of these techniques. There is no good evidence that 
indicates that these more sophisticated tools are more useful than 
optimising standard CT parameters.
