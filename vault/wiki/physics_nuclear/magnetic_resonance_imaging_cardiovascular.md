MAGNETIC RESONANCE IMAGING
The use of MRI in thoracic imaging is slowly expanding from a problem-
solving and research tool for chest wall and mediastinal problems into 
routine clinical practice. The general advantages of MRI are its excellent 
soft-tissue contrast and lack of ionising radiation. However, in the lungs 
these advantages have historically been outweighed by significant limit-
ing factors: the low signal-to-noise ratio due to proton paucity of lung 
parenchyma, breathing and respiratory motion artefact exacerbated by 
long acquisition times and very short T2* decay times caused by the 
heterogeneous magnetic susceptibility of the pulmonary anatomical 
interfaces.
SUMMARY BOX
•	 MRI remains most useful for mediastinal and chest wall lesion characterisation
•	 Technological advancements in thoracic MRI, especially single-shot tech-
niques, now permit single-breathold MR visualisation of the entire lung
•	 The use of MRI with hyperpolarised gases can increasingly help identify 
functional decline in patients with ventilator impairment
capabilities of MRI are making exciting additions to our understanding 
of pulmonary disease. Historically, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) 
of the thorax was considered impractical due to respiratory and cardiac 
movement. However, application of more advanced gating techniques 
and adaptations to the cyclical, and therefore predictable, nature of 
artefacts, has partially overcome this limitation. Whilst best studied in 
highly cellular lesions, such as lymphoma and small cell malignancy, 
studies have shown DWI sequences can differentiate histological subtypes 
in malignant pleural mesothelioma, and are being evaluated for thoracic 
nodal staging for non-small cell lung cancer.
The use of inhaled gas contrast agents has several research applica-
tions. Hyperpolarised (3He and 129Xe) and fluorinated gases can be 
imaged using fast imaging sequences, such as GRE and FLASH, obtained 
in a single breath-hold. Ventilation maps derived from such techniques 
have been shown to reveal subclinical decline in lung function, and 
provide insight into the regional variability of obstructive lung diseases. 
DWI imaging of gas agents provides semiquantitative assessment of 
alveolar volume, a technique which has been recently applied to quantify 
in vivo alveolar surface area. This has revolutionised the understanding 
of alveolar development, demonstrating lung growth persisting through-
out adolescence. The clinical implementation of such techniques is, 
however, significantly limited by the cost and infrastructure required 
in producing and imaging hyperpolarised gases.
Bottled medical oxygen can also be used as an MRI contrast agent due 
to the paramagnetic properties of its dissolved form. Although limited 
by relatively long imaging times, it is a readily available technique and 
does not require the intensive support structure of hyperpolarised gas 
imaging. Oxygen-enhanced MRI (OE-MRI) of the lungs has been applied 
to several respiratory diseases (Fig. 1.12) to perform measurements of 
alveolar ventilation. Fourier decomposition MRI is another promising 
and readily implementable technique performed in free breathing. This 
technique extracts the periodic voxel-by-voxel signal variations caused 
by respiration and cardiac pulsatility to derive non-contrast ventilation 
and pulmonary perfusion maps. This technique has been evaluated in 
patients with various conditions, including cystic fibrosis, in order to 
derive automated measures of ventilation and perfusion defects.
There is increasing need for 3D dynamic airways imaging and whilst 
CT can certainly provide high spatial and temporal resolution images 
of airway mechanics, cine MRI imaging is beginning to emerge as a 
viable non-ionising radiation-dependant alternative for dynamic assess-
ment of the central airways.

Ventilation–Perfusion Scintigraphy
Ventilation–perfusion (V/Q) scintigraphy is a non-invasive technique 
for the assessment of the distribution of pulmonary blood flow and 
alveolar ventilation and has primarily been used for the diagnosis of 
pulmonary embolism. Lung scintigraphy remains part of the diagnostic 
algorithm in the investigation of patients with pulmonary embolism. 
Guidelines suggest that it may be considered, subject to its availability, 
as the initial imaging investigation provided the chest radiograph is 
Ongoing technological advancements have, however, partially miti-
gated these limitations. Single-shot techniques (vendor-specific acronyms 
include HASTE and FASE) permit k-space to be sufficiently filled in a 
single breath-hold, providing T2 weighted imaging. In addition, 3D low 
flip angle gradient recalled echo (GRE) sequences (an example being 
volume-interpolated breath-hold examination [VIBE]) are also sufficiently 
fast and provide T1 weighted images, with or without fat saturation.
Additionally, respiratory triggering with or without electrocardio-
graphic (ECG) gating (single or double triggering) of image sequences 
can further mitigate the effect of cardiovascular and respiratory move-
ment. In recent years the use of navigator echoes to track diaphragmatic 
motion has become the most popular method for performing respiratory 
triggering.
There are several accepted indications for thoracic MRI. T2 weighted 
images can confirm the cystic nature of mediastinal lesions that appear 
solid on CT (cysts containing non-serous fluid can have high attenuation 
on CT). T1 weighted images enhanced with gadolinium-based contrast 
agents have been shown to outperform CT imaging in assessing dia-
phragmatic invasion of pleural mesothelioma. Fat-saturation sequences, 
such as short-tau inversion recovery (STIR) sequences, may outperform 
FDG-PET (fluorodeoxyglucose-PET) in nodal staging of non-small cell 
lung cancer and have utility in differentiating fat and haemorrhage in 
the evaluation of mediastinal masses. Fat detection using chemical shift 
imaging can be useful in differentiating rebound thymic hyperplasia (Fig. 
1.11) from recurrent malignancy or anterior mediastinal metastases.
Although not yet in routine clinical practice, ultrashort (UTE) and 
zero (ZTE) echo-time pulse sequences apply readout gradients imme-
diately after or during the radiofrequency pulse, combined with radial 
k-space mapping. This permits tissues with very short T2* decay (as 
low as 0.5 ms), such as the lung parenchyma itself, to return detectable 
signal. Whilst these techniques remain inferior to CT for structural 
assessment of pulmonary parenchymal lesions, the quantitative imaging

scintigraphy (VQ), usually combined with low-dose CT, is now preferred to

•	 Positron emission tomography (PET), hybridised with CT and increasingly

Overview of idiopathic interstitial pneumonias
• The lung has a limited repertoire of responses to injury. Common responses to injury 
range from recruiting lymphocytes or macrophages, increasing inflammatory debris, 
and instigating a fibrotic reaction.
• The idiopathic interstitial pneumonias (IIPs) are seven different patterns of injury 
response. These patterns can be associated with collagen vascular disease, drug 
reaction, occupational exposure, or they may be idiopathic.
• A disease can only be classified as an IIP if there is no other explanation for the 
pathologic changes. 
• Each of the seven entities has both a clinical syndrome and a separate pathologic 
diagnosis. Sometimes the clinical syndrome and the pathologic terms have different 
names and sometimes they share the same name, but in every case there is an 
acronym!
• In order to understand the histopathological responses to injury, it is important to 
review the normal alveolar anatomy.

Lymphoid interstitial pneumonia (LIP) is both a clinical syndrome and the pathologic
diagnosis. LIP is exceptionally rare as an isolated idiopathic disease and is more
commonly associated with Sjögren syndrome or HIV.
•
The histologic hallmark of LIP is diffuse infiltration of the interstitium by lymphocytes
and other immune cells, with resultant distortion of the alveoli.

Imaging findings of LIP include diffuse or lower-lobe predominant ground glass.

due to air trapping from peribronchiolar cellular debris. LIP may be complicated by
pneumothorax in advanced disease.
