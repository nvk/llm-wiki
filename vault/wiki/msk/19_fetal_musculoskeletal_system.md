The standard obstetrical ultrasound examination delineated 
in the ACR-ACOG-AIUM-SRU Practice Parameter for the 
Performance of Obstetrical Ultrasound incorporates imaging 
of the fetal musculoskeletal system, including the femur 
length (FL); the calvarium during measurement of the bipa-
rietal diameter and head circumference; and the spine, legs, 
and arms. When a skeletal abnormality is suspected, more 
detailed views and additional measurements are obtained as 
appropriate.
MUSCULOSKELETAL ABNORMALITIES
Head and Spine
Abnormal mineralization of the fetal calvarium can be 
detected with ultrasound. The normal fetal calvarium is 
highly echogenic due to mineralization, resulting in rever-
beration artifact on the side of the brain closest to the trans-
ducer. This artifact interferes with sonographic visualization 
of the underlying intracranial structures (Fig. 19-1A). Abnor-
mally decreased calvarial mineralization facilitates better-
than-expected sonographic visualization of the near-ﬁeld 
brain (see Fig. 19-1B; Video 19-1). Because the demineralized 
calvarium is soft, mild transducer pressure can result in tran-
sient distortion of the calvarial contour.
A variety of abnormal calvarial shapes are detected at ante-
natal ultrasound. A cloverleaf conﬁguration is a deformity 
caused by craniosynostosis of all the sutures (Fig. 19-2A). It 
is seen in fetuses with type II thanatophoric dysplasia, in 
other skeletal dysplasias and syndromes, and as an isolated 
abnormality. A lemon-shaped skull is a commonly identiﬁed 
conﬁguration due to ﬂattening or concavity of the frontal 
bone and is found in fetuses with Chiari II malformation as 
well as in some normal fetuses (see Fig. 19-2B). Strawberry-
shaped skull refers to ﬂattening of the occiput and ﬂattening 
or pointing of the frontal bone, and is seen in some fetuses 
with trisomy 18 (see Fig. 19-2C). Frontal bossing is an unusu-
ally prominent protruding forehead and is best visualized on 
a sagittal proﬁle view of the head (see Fig. 19-2D). Frontal 
bossing occurs in skeletal dysplasias such as achondropla-
sia, aneuploidies, syndromes, and hydrocephalus, as well as 
secondary to premature synostosis of the lambdoid suture. 
Brachycephaly refers to a disproportionately wide head. 
Brachycephaly can be a normal variant but is also seen in the 
setting of premature bilateral coronal suture synostosis, syn-
dromes, and skeletal dysplasias such as achondrogenesis, 
as well as in children with trisomy 21 (see Fig. 19-2E). 
Dolichocephaly is a disproportionately long head and can be 
a normal variant but is also found in the setting of craniosyn-
ostosis of the sagittal suture, breech presentation, and in the 
setting of oligohydramnios (see Fig. 19-2F and G).
Sonography depicts a wide range of fetal spine abnormali-
ties. Spina biﬁda with meningocele or myelomeningocele and 
Chiari II malformation is a commonly detected fetal spine 
abnormality (see Figs. 15-46 to 15-49). Scoliosis, kyphosis, 
and other spine deformities due to vertebral segmentation 
abnormalities during embryogenesis can also be detected at 
ultrasound (Fig. 19-3). Spine anomalies occur as isolated 
defects and in the setting of skeletal dysplasias and syndromes 
such as VACTERL (vertebral, anal, cardiac, tracheal, esopha-
geal, renal, and limb anomalies). Spine abnormalities in skel-
etal dysplasias include demineralization, in which one or 
more of the ossiﬁcation centers are not echogenic, and 
platyspondyly, which is characterized by ﬂattening of the 
vertebral bodies. Additional spine abnormalities such as 
sacrococcygeal teratoma and caudal regression are discussed 
further in Chapter 15.
Hands and Feet
Antenatal ultrasound depicts a wide range of hand and foot 
abnormalities. These include isolated abnormalities as well as 
those associated with other disorders. The normal fetal hands 
and feet move freely. An open view of the hand is helpful in 
visualizing the bony structures including metacarpals, meta-
tarsals, and phalanges (Fig. 19-4). The open-hand view opti-
mally includes all the outstretched ﬁngers in a single ﬁeld of 
view, although the thumb is often seen by itself or in conjunc-
tion with one or more of the other digits in a slightly different 
scan plane.
Polydactyly is deﬁned as more than ﬁve ﬁngers or toes and 
can be an isolated ﬁnding, familial, as well as associated with 
syndromes, skeletal dysplasias, and aneuploidy, particularly 
trisomy 13 (Fig. 19-5; Video 19-2). Postaxial polydactyly 
refers to extra digits on the ulnar side of the hand, adjacent 
to the little ﬁnger, and is more common than preaxial poly-
dactyly, which refers to extra digits on the radial side. An 
extra digit may include a complete digit with soft tissues and 
bones or only a rudimentary projection of soft tissue that may 
be difﬁcult to visualize with antenatal ultrasound.
Multiple additional hand and foot abnormalities can be 
identiﬁed in utero. Clinodactyly is persistent inward curva-
ture of a digit (Fig. 19-6A) and is often due to a hypoplastic 
middle phalanx, a ﬁnding that has been associated with 
trisomy 21 when the ﬁfth digit is involved. Clinodactyly is 
also found in other aneuploidies and as an isolated ﬁnding in 
normal fetuses. Ectrodactyly (also termed split hand or split 
foot) refers to absence of one or more central digits in the

The differential diagnosis for a short femur includes a con-
stitutionally short but normal fetus (frequently the offspring 
of short parents), skeletal dysplasia, fetal growth restriction, 
and aneuploidy (Box 19-1). Constitutionally short normal 
fetuses typically exhibit only mild femoral shortening, appro-
priate interval growth, and no structural abnormalities (Fig. 
19-12A and B). Fetuses with short femur on the basis of 
growth restriction can often be distinguished from those with 
skeletal dysplasia by relatively mild FL shortening, small 
abdominal circumference (AC), and absence of additional 
skeletal abnormalities (see Fig. 19-12C and D). In the uncom-
mon case of growth restriction in which the femurs are mark-
edly short, distinction between growth restriction and skeletal 
dysplasia can be challenging and may require follow-up 
studies before a conﬁdent diagnosis can be made. 
Oligohydramnios and decreased soft-tissue thickness are 
more likely in growth-restricted fetuses, whereas polyhy-
dramnios and thick redundant skinfolds are more common in 
fetuses with skeletal dysplasia, because growth of the skin and 
soft tissues is often unaffected in skeletal dysplasia even 
though skeletal growth is restricted (see Fig. 19-12E and F). 
Fetuses with skeletal dysplasias frequently have additional 
major skeletal abnormalities and very short FLs, often con-
siderably more than four standard deviations below the mean.
