# Infiltrative (Interstitial) Lung Disease

**Domain:** [[cardio/_index|Cardiothoracic Radiology]] ([Cardiothoracic Radiology](_index.md))  
**Source:** Thoracic Radiology: The Requisites (Pages 40–43)  

## Summary & Core Concepts
There are many causes of parenchymal consolidation. The process may be localized or diffuse. R and 10L, tracheobronchial; 11R and 11L, bronchopulmonary (hilar) nodes.

## Key Radiological & Clinical Findings
- **Radiography (Plain Film):** Essential baseline imaging for Infiltrative (Interstitial) Lung Disease; evaluates anatomical contours, alignment, lucencies, and gross calcifications.
- **Computed Tomography (CT):** tified in infiltrative lung disease on CT.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** High soft-tissue resolution with exquisite contrast resolution for soft tissues, marrow space, and fluid kinetics.
- **Ultrasound / Doppler:** Real-time, non-ionizing dynamic assessment with color and spectral Doppler evaluation of vascular flow.

## Differential Diagnosis & High-Yield Pitfalls
- Diagnostic mimics sharing radiographic or cross-sectional morphology with Infiltrative (Interstitial) Lung Disease.
- Key discriminator: Contrast enhancement patterns, tissue composition (fat, calcium, blood products), and anatomical distribution.
- Clinical correlation: Integration with patient presentation, laboratory biomarkers, and acuity dictates definitive diagnostic pathway.

The interstitial space consists of two major anatomic
compartments: the axial interstitial space and the paren­
chymal interstitial space. The former surrounds the bron­
chovascular bundles and occurs primarily in the central
portions of the lung. The axial interstitium also extends out
to the level of the terminal bronchioles. The ­parenchymal
interstitial space is between the alveolar and capillary base­
ment membranes in the peripheral portions of the lung
(i.e., alveolar walls). Many interstitial or infiltrative lung
diseases involve both compartments; interstitial pulmo­
nary edema is a classic example (Fig. 1-29). The interlobu­
lar septa contain the veins and lymphatics. Thickening of
the interlobular septa produces the classic short subpleural
lines that lie perpendicular to the pleural space. The septal
lines are also called B lines of Kerley.
Two major patterns of small opacities can be identified
in interstitial lung disease: linear and nodular. The linear
pattern may befine, medium, or coarse. The nodular pat­
tern consists of small, rounded opacities that are less than
1 cm in diameter (Fig. 1-30). Nodules 1 to 2 mm in diame­
ter are sometimes to referred to as a miliary or micronodular

Atelectasis
Atelectasis is a decrease in the volume of a lung or a por­
tion of the lung. Atelectasis may be referred to as collapse,
although this definition is somewhat simplistic. Several
types of atelectasis are related to the mechanism by which
the loss in lung volume occurs (Box 1-8). The most com­
mon type is caused by central bronchial obstruction that
usually leads to lobar or, less frequently, segmental col­
lapse. When an obstruction occurs, gas is resorbed from
the alveoli.
