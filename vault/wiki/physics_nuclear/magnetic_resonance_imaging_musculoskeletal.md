SECTION C  The Musculoskeletal System
MAGNETIC RESONANCE IMAGING
Routine clinical MR in the musculoskeletal system depends on a limited 
number of standard fast-spin echo and gradient-echo sequences. These 
are selected to best differentiate musculoskeletal tissues. The gains made 
in recent years have been in the form of increased spatial resolution, 
affording better anatomical detail, by using higher field strength magnets 
and improved coil design. As a result, we are beginning to see invasive 
routine MR arthrography replaced by conventional MRI. A handful of 
other sequences such as diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), motion 
reduction MR, ultrashort echo time (TE) (Fig. 38.7), and cartilage 
mapping are gaining traction, whereas dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI 
(DCE-MRI) has not yet been found to be useful.
There are no standard protocols for the MR imaging of joints but 
there are widely agreed principles. Proton density (PD) and T2 weighted 
(TW2) sequences are favoured for assessing fibrocartilage, such as the 
menisci, and ligaments and tendons. PD sequences, which often are 
fat-saturated, are preferred for hyaline cartilage. T1 weighted (TW1) and 
fat-saturated T2 weighted sequences are used for bone marrow. Each 
joint should be imaged in all three planes using a combination of these.
There are further modifications to these broad principles that can be 
helpful. Fat-saturated T2 weighted images can be optimised by not using 
very long echo times. A TE of 60 to 70 ms is long enough to ensure that 
bone marrow oedema is visible but generates much more signal-to-noise 
than a TE of 120 ms. Intermediate weighted MR—halfway between a 
PD and a T2 weighted—with a TE of about 35 ms can be useful for 
separating hyaline cartilage, fibrocartilage and synovial fluid. MR imaging 
of the ankle is prone to inhomogeneous fat suppression because the long

COMPUTED TOMOGRAPHY
The most common indications for CT in the musculoskeletal system 
are as extensions to conventional radiography of fractures and follow-up 
of orthopaedic implants, where three-dimensional complexity cannot 
be adequately presented on a planar image. CT also has a valuable role 
in guiding intervention.
Dual-Energy Computed Tomography
The diagnosis of crystal arthropathies in the appendicular skeleton 
can be assisted with dual-energy CT (DECT). DECT can be used 
to differentiate calcium salts by their relative attenuation of x-rays 
produced at two different peak voltages, typically 80 and 140 kVp 
(peak kilovoltage). This is achieved on modern CT machines by rapid 
alternating ‘kV switching,’ between the two voltages, on a single-source 
and single-detector system. This can be done in a single acquisition, 
ensuring that the two data sets are perfectly co-registered. Originally 
developed to identify the composition of renal stones, its use has 
been extended to the detection of soft-tissue urate deposition in gout, 
differentiating uric acid crystals from other causes of soft-tissue calcifica-
tion which otherwise look identical on plain radiography or routine 
diagnostic CT. This can be particularly useful when serum urate levels 
are normal in the acute phase of the disease, and when joint aspira-
tion is ‘dry’ and it is too early for other radiographic features to have 
appeared.
Metal Artefact Reduction
DECT can also be used to reduce beam-hardening artefact around 
orthopaedic prostheses, although there are a number of techniques 
employed to this end. Metal implants cause disproportionate attenuation 
of x-ray photons at the lower end of the spectrum. The filtered back-
projection technique used to reconstruct the CT image from the detector 
data assumes a relatively even attenuation at all energies and this results 
in the beam-hardening artefact. This effect can be ameliorated by 
increasing the kVp. This reduces the proportion of lower-energy photons 
being attenuated but it comes at a cost of increased dose of radiation 
to the patient. Monoenergetic reconstruction of DECT data can be 
performed to create images that are derived from a single virtual kVp 
(Fig. 38.6). While increasing this monoenergetic kVp does reduce 
beam-hardening artefact, it comes at a cost of an increase in noise.
Noise around metal implants results from photon starvation because 
a large proportion of the x-ray beam is attenuated. This can be addressed 
by decreasing the pitch settings, narrowing the collimation and increasing 
mA. Again, this comes with an increased radiation burden. The effect 
of the noise can be minimised during post-processing and MPR by 
increasing the reconstructed slice thickness or using slice averaging 
techniques.
There are a number of vendor-specific products that are marketed 
for metal artefact reduction in CT. Some of these use iterative reconstruc-
tion techniques instead of back-projection. Others using mask or 
segmentation techniques to model and correct for the metal object, or 
a combination of these techniques. There is no good evidence that 
indicates that these more sophisticated tools are more useful than 
optimising standard CT parameters.

MR imaging of small joints presents further challenges. MR machines 
and standard wrist and ankle coils can have problems dealing with the 
small volume of signal being generated by extremities. To overcome 
this, fingers or toes can be placed on a saline bag, or bespoke small 
parts coils can be used. Slice thickness needs to be reduced to a minimum 
and inter-slice gaps removed to produce a contiguous dataset. Chemical-
shift artefact can badly degrade images at very small fields of view, 
particularly at higher field strengths. This can be addressed by increasing 
the receiver bandwidth and resolution at the cost of reducing the 
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) (Fig. 38.8).
Metal Artefact Reduction
MRI is prone to artefacts induced by metal implants when they become 
magnetised, distorting the homogeneity of the B0 magnetic field and 
leading to both signal loss from dephasing and misregistration arte-
facts along the frequency encoding gradient. Misregistration produces 
geometric distortion and summation artefacts. Geometric distortion 
occurs when the signal is attributed to the incorrect location on the 
image matrix. Summation artefacts produce either high or low signal 
lines around the implant where data from one anatomical location is 
added to data at a different location in the matrix. These geometric 
and summation misregistration artefacts occur both in the plane of the 
image (in-plane) and from plane to adjacent plane (through-plane).
There are three broad approaches to minimising the effects of metal 
susceptibility artefacts: the positioning and composition of the prosthesis; 
the optimisation of standard MR parameters; and the use of advanced 
vendor-specific metal artefact reduction sequences.
It is much easier to reduce artefact from titanium implants than 
from steel alloys. Long thin prostheses that lie parallel to the long axis 
of the B0 field produce less artefact than those lying perpendicular. The 
relative orientation of these cannot be changed but the position of 
metalwork in the ankle and wrist can sometimes be optimised through 
patient positioning. Spherical prostheses produce the most artefact and 
so large bearing hip replacements and knee replacements are the most 
difficult to image with MR. Susceptibility artefact is most prominent 
in the frequency encoding direction and therefore judicious exchange 
of the frequency and phase encoding directions can be used to ‘steer’ 
artefact away from areas of interest. Long-echo T2 weighted and gradient-
echo sequences are particularly prone to dephasing and therefore T1 
and PD fast-spin echo sequences are preferred. STIR sequences are 
much more reliable than spectral fat-suppressed T2 imaging.
Increasing the receiver bandwidth and decreasing the voxel size will 
reduce the effects of susceptibility within the plane of the image. Reducing 
slice thickness will have a similar effect on through-plane artefacts. 
While this comes at a cost of a decrease in SNR, in practice the benefits 
to image quality outweigh the risks (Fig. 38.9).
There are a number of vendor-specific metal artefact reduction 
sequences that use a variety of multispectral imaging techniques, some 
in combination with view-angle tilting, to produce substantial reduction 
in artefact. These techniques rely on replacing frequency with phase 
encoding, reconstructing from 3D datasets, and skewing the readout 
gradients to compensate for frequency misregistration. The general 
limitations of the techniques include some blurring of the images, fixed 
matrix sizes and limited availability of different weightings.

Talar fracture
•
Fractures of the talus can be divided anatomically into lateral process, posterior 
process, head, body, and neck fractures.
•
Fractures of the talar neck are classified by the Hawkins classification. A talar neck 
fracture may disrupt the blood supply to the talus and predispose to osteonecrosis.
•
The Hawkins sign describes a subchondral lucent band seen on the frontal ankle
radiograph 6–8 weeks after ankle immobilization. The lucent band represents increased 
bone reabsorption from active hyperemia. The presence of the Hawkins sign implies an
intact blood supply to the talar dome and is a good prognostic indicator in talar neck
fractures. Absence of the Hawkins sign suggests avascular necrosis.

Hindfoot (tarsal) coalition

The two major forms of anomalous anatomy are described as either Partial (PAPVR) or 
Total (TAPVR) anomalous pulmonary venous return. In both forms, pulmonary veins drain into the 
systemic system - not the left atrium. Taking blood with oxygen (pulmonary vein blood) which should 
normally go into the aorta and dumping it back into the right sided of the heart (left to right shunt) has 
the potential to overload that system and potentially cause pulmonary hypertension / right heart 
failure (depending on the severity of the shunt).

consequence. However, there are associations which can cause problems (and are highly testable).

Pulmonary Infarct Mimics: A pulmonary infarct is a wedge-shaped opacity that is going to 
“melt” (resolve slowly), and sometimes can cavitate. Obviously a cavitary lesion throws up lots of 
flags and makes people say TB, or cancer. When it’s an opacity in the lung and the patient doesn’t 
have a fever, sometimes people think cancer - plenty of pulmonary infarcts have been biopsied.
Pulmonary Veno-Occlusive Disease: Uncommon variant of primary pulmonary 
hypertension, that affects the post capillary pulmonary vasculature. For gamesmanship: PAH + 
Normal Wedge, you should think this. The normal wedge pressure differentiates it from other post 
capillary causes; such as left atrial myxoma, mitral stenosis, and pulmonary vein stenosis.
Pulmonary Artery Aneurysm/Pseudoaneurysm_- Think about three things for

The buzzwords for Behcets are: “Turkish descent” and “mouth and genital ulcers.”
• Hughes-Stovin Syndrome: This is a zebra cause of pulmonary artery aneurysm that is similar 
(and maybe the same thing) as Behcets. It is characterized by recurrent thrombophlebitis and 
pulmonary artery aneurysm formation and rupture.
• Rasmussen Aneurysm: This has a cool name, which instantly makes it high yield for testing.
This is a pulmonary artery pseudoaneurysm secondary to pulmonary TB. It usually involves 
the upper lobes in the setting of reactivation TB.
• Tetralogy of Fallot Repair Gone South: So another possible testable scenario is the patch 
aneurysm, from the RVOT repair.
Pulmonary Hypertension - Pulmonary arterial pressures over 25 are going to make the 
diagnosis. I prefer to use the “outdated” primary and secondary way of thinking about this.
Primary: Idiopathic type is very uncommon, seen in a small group of young women in their 20s.
Secondary: This is by far the majority, and there are a few causes you need to know: Chronic PE , 
Right Heart Failure/ Strain, Lung Parenchymal Problems- (This would include emphysema, and 
various causes of fibrosis). COPDers with a pulmonary artery bigger than the aorta (A/PA ratio) 
have increased mortality (says the NEJM).

signs include right ventricular dilation / hypertrophy, and centrilobular ground-glass nodules.
