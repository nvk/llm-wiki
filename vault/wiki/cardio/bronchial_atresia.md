# Bronchial Atresia

**Domain:** [[cardio/_index|Cardiothoracic Radiology]] ([Cardiothoracic Radiology](_index.md))  
**Source:** Thoracic Radiology: The Requisites (Pages 72–75)  

## Summary & Core Concepts
Congenital tracheal stenosis. Tracheomalacia is shown on paired inspiratory and dynamic expiratory axial CT images. A, On inspiration, the trachea is of normal caliber.

## Key Radiological & Clinical Findings
- **Radiography (Plain Film):** The anteroposterior radiograph shows an overinflated left upper lobe with a slight mediastinal shift.
- **Computed Tomography (CT):** Tracheomalacia is shown on paired inspiratory and dynamic expiratory axial CT images. B, CT confirms the large tracheal lumen.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** High soft-tissue resolution with exquisite contrast resolution for soft tissues, marrow space, and fluid kinetics.
- **Ultrasound / Doppler:** Real-time, non-ionizing dynamic assessment with color and spectral Doppler evaluation of vascular flow.

## Differential Diagnosis & High-Yield Pitfalls
- Diagnostic mimics sharing radiographic or cross-sectional morphology with Bronchial Atresia.
- Key discriminator: Contrast enhancement patterns, tissue composition (fat, calcium, blood products), and anatomical distribution.
- Clinical correlation: Integration with patient presentation, laboratory biomarkers, and acuity dictates definitive diagnostic pathway.

Bronchopulmonary isomerism has an identical pattern of

Bronchial Atresia
This anomaly consists of atresia of a lobar or segmental
bronchus with obliteration of the lumen and preservation
of distal structures. The most common site is the left upper
lobe, particularly the apical posterior segment. The right
middle and upper lobes are less common sites. Mucus
secreted within the airways distal to the atretic segment
cannot pass the stenosis and accumulates as a mucous plug
or mucocele. Collateral air drift keeps the lobe or segment
inflated, and it becomes hyperinflated as a result of expi­
ratory air trapping. The chest radiograph shows an area of
hyperlucency in the affected portion of the lung (Fig. 2-6).

Congenital Bronchiectasis
Congenital bronchiectasis (i.e., Williams-Campbell syn­
drome) is rare, and its existence is controversial. It results
from an intrinsic abnormality of cartilage. The cartilagi­
nous deficiency occurs within the fourth- to sixth-order
bronchi and is manifested by cystic bronchiectasis and pul­
monary hyperinflation (Fig. 2-8). Bronchiectasis, which is
acquired and caused by chronic infection, may occur early
in life as a result of other congenital, developmental, or
genetic disorders. These conditions are listed in Box 2-2
(see Chapter 13).
LUNGS
Pulmonary Agenesis and Hypoplasia
Pulmonary agenesis is a condition with total absence
of the lung parenchyma and the vessels and bronchi
­distal to the carina. Patients with pulmonary aplasia have
a ­rudimentary bronchus, which ends in a blind pouch
­without lung tissue or pulmonary vasculature. A decrease
in the number or size of airways, vessels, and alveoli
characterizes pulmonary hypoplasia. The lesion may be
primary, or it may result from several pathogenetic mecha­
nisms that may occur during gestation. These conditions
include decreased pulmonary vascular perfusion, oligohy­
dramnios, or compression of the lung by a space-occupying
mass within the pleural cavity (e.g., congenital diaphrag­
matic hernia).
