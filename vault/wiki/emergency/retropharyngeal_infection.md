# Retropharyngeal Infection

**Domain:** [[emergency/_index|Emergency Radiology]] ([Emergency Radiology](_index.md))  
**Source:** Emergency Radiology: The Requisites (Pages 61–62)  

## Summary & Core Concepts
Normal occipitomastoid suture. Normal tympanosquamous suture. Retropharyngeal abscess.

## Key Radiological & Clinical Findings
- **Radiography (Plain Film):** Essential baseline imaging for Retropharyngeal Infection; evaluates anatomical contours, alignment, lucencies, and gross calcifications.
- **Computed Tomography (CT):** Gold-standard modality for multiplanar delineation, density characterization, and staging of Retropharyngeal Infection.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** High soft-tissue resolution with exquisite contrast resolution for soft tissues, marrow space, and fluid kinetics.
- **Ultrasound / Doppler:** Real-time, non-ionizing dynamic assessment with color and spectral Doppler evaluation of vascular flow.

## Differential Diagnosis & High-Yield Pitfalls
- Diagnostic mimics sharing radiographic or cross-sectional morphology with Retropharyngeal Infection.
- Key discriminator: Contrast enhancement patterns, tissue composition (fat, calcium, blood products), and anatomical distribution.
- Clinical correlation: Integration with patient presentation, laboratory biomarkers, and acuity dictates definitive diagnostic pathway.

Retropharyngeal Infection
Spread of pharyngeal infection to the medial or lateral
­(Rouvière) retropharyngeal nodes may lead to cellulitis
or development of suppurative adenitis (enlarged, ­ rim-
­enhancing node). Associated edema of the ­retropharyngeal
soft ­ tissues is quite common, noted by expansion and
­diffusely increased density of the retropharyngeal fat.
Extracapsular spread of nodal infection may result in
a true ­ retropharyngeal abscess typically identified as a
rim-­enhancing bow-tie-shapedfluid collection. Retro­
pharyngeal space infections may quickly spread into the
­mediastinum (Fig. 1-58), which is a serious complication
with high mortality and morbidity. Immediate surgical
drainage of a retropharyngeal abscess may be indicated.
Imaging plays an important role in determining the indica­
tion and access route for surgical drainage. Vascular com­
plications of infection include spasm, arteritis and mycotic
aneurysm of the internal carotid artery, and thrombophle­
bitis of the internal jugular vein (Lemierre syndrome).
The latter condition was more common in the preantibi­
otic era, with rare cases still encountered in the present
day. On postcontrast CT or MR imaging, afilling defect in
the internal jugular vein with enhancement of the vessel
wall can be demonstrated. Early diagnosis is critical, as the
untreated thrombus can propagate systemically, resulting
in septicemia and pulmonary emboli/infarcts.
In the setting of peritonsillar and retropharyngeal in­
fections, careful examination of the airway is vital. Pro­
phylactic intubation should be considered if the airway is
­significantly compromised.
