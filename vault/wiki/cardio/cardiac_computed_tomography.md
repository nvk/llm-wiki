Cardiac Computed 
Tomography
Amgad N. Makaryus and Lawrence M. Boxt
The clinical potential of computed tomography (CT) of the 
heart had always been limited by relatively long acquisition 
time and poor spatial resolution. The introduction of spiral 
multidetector CT scanning, and in particular, achievement of 
isotropic voxel acquisition, has shortened acquisition time, in-
creased spatial and contrast resolution, and resulted in a dra-
matic expansion of the use of CT for evaluation of patients 
with acquired and congenital heart disease. Cardiac diagnosis 
by CT had always been limited to identiﬁ cation of abnormal 
calciﬁ cation, or the observation of incidental ﬁ ndings, such as 
pleural or pericardial effusion. CT diagnosis and the character-
ization of pericardial, myocardial, and valvular disease, as well 
as the cause and sequelae of ischemic coronary heart disease, 
are now not only possible, but are rapidly becoming an imaging 
modality of choice in certain patient populations and clinical 
circumstances.
CURRENT TECHNOLOGY
CT scanners acquire a series of rotating thinly collimated 
x-ray beam projections obtained at different angles around the 
circumference of the patient (raw data). Individual axial im-
ages (slices) are formed from these projection data by the 
mathematical technique of back projection. The quality of an 
image formed by this technique improves with increasing the 
number of projections and by overlapping projection data. 
The number of x-ray projections obtained for image formation 
is limited by the ability of the scanner to rotate the x-ray 
source around the patient. That is, the faster the tube rotates 
around the patient, the shorter interval between individual 
projections, and thus, the more projections available for image 
formation per tube rotation. Use of a number of detectors 
(multidetector CT) and passing the patient through the bore 
of the scanner allows acquisition of a series of slices (a stack), 
a three-dimensional array of image data, which may be used 
for reconstruction of oblique sections and three-dimensional 
volume-rendered images.
Electrocardiogram (ECG) gated CT acquisition dramatically 
improves image quality. The ECG signal obtained from the 
patient provides a time code, indicating to the CT scanner 
where in the temporal progression of the cardiac cycle each of 
the projection data is obtained. That is, the scanner knows 
where in space projection data is obtained. The ECG signal 
assigns a time coordinate to the three-dimensional space co-
ordinates of the image data, allowing the data to be parsed 
based on time and space (i.e., a series of stacks of image data, 
each obtained at one point in the cardiac cycle). This has two 
important advantages. First, it signiﬁ cantly decreases motion 
artifacts in cardiac imagery. Because image formation is per-
formed using only projection data obtained at a particular phase 
of the cardiac cycle, imagery is coherent and appears sharper. 
Second, acquisition of a series of image stacks allows analysis of 
changes in cardiac morphology over the cardiac cycle, thus, 
providing a means of evaluating cardiac function.
APPROACH TO CARDIAC COMPUTED 
TOMOGRAPHY
Indications for Cardiac Computed Tomography
Indications for 64-detector cardiac CT are predominantly for 
evaluation of the epicardial coronary arteries in speciﬁ c circum-
stances related to patient signs and symptoms and risk for coro-
nary heart disease. Nevertheless, the value of CT in general, 
enhanced by the high temporal and spatial resolution of isotropic 
voxel acquisition (i.e., ECG gated 64-detector scanning) make 
use of this modality attractive in any patient in whom cardiac 
disease is being evaluated and especially if a CT is considered for 
evaluation of the heart or mediastinum.
Patient Preparation
Cardiac CT requires administration of intravenous iodinated 
contrast, necessitating prior evaluation of renal function and 
history of previous contrast administration complications. Of 
particular importance for performing cardiac CT is heart rate 
control. Slow, regular heart rate is essential for reliable data acqui-
sition, image reconstruction, and accurate and conﬁ dent interpre-
tation. Thus, assessing the patients’ ability to receive rate control 
medications such as beta-blockers also needs to be considered. 
For example, patients with severe bronchospastic asthma should 
not receive beta-blockers, but they may receive other rate-
control medications such as calcium channel blockers. Unless 
contraindicated, preexamination oral metoprolol may be ade-
quate to achieve a target heart rate of 60 beats per minute. Intra-
venous administration of a series of 5-mg boluses of metoprolol 
immediately before examination, or while the patient is on the 
scanner table, may be necessary (or preferred, depending on the 
environment in which the examination is performed [i.e., inpa-
tient versus outpatient facility]).
Examination is rapid enough to allow acquisition of all image 
data within a single breath hold. However, timing of the breath 
hold, and the depth of inspiration used is critical, and therefore, 
the patient must be informed of this, and better, allowed to prac-
tice before examination. Finally, cardiac CT examination is a fast 
procedure, and the patient undergoing the examination will ex-
perience a series of loud noises (gantry rotation) and unusual 
sensations (the ﬂ ush of contrast administration) in rather rapid 
progression. Therefore, adequate explanation of what to expect 
during the examination will pay off in a more relaxed patient and 
more reliable imagery for interpretation.
