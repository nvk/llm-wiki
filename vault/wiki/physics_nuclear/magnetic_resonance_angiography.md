Although intermittent claudication and ischaemic rest pain represent 
the opposite ends of the spectrum of peripheral vascular disease, 
atherosclerosis tends to affect the peripheral vessels at multiple locations, 
and regardless of which of these two syndromes the patient presents 
with, modern imaging with CTA and MRA is performed from the 
abdominal aorta to the pedal arteries owing to multiplicity of lesions. 
Although muscle ischaemia can be evaluated with a variety of techniques 
such as dynamic postcontrast imaging, DWI and spectroscopy, this is 
rarely helpful in clinical practice (Fig. 77.14).
Preintervention Imaging With Computed Tomography 
Angiography and Magnetic Resonance Angiography
The aim of imaging is to determine the extent of disease, patency of 
arterial segments, multiplicity of lesions and potential route for accessing 
diseased segments (the roadmap). This can be achieved with either CTA 
and MRA, albeit with different approaches, advantages and limitations.
COMPUTED TOMOGRAPHY ANGIOGRAPHY
For CTA, imaging of the peripheral arteries simply requires continued 
table movement to cover the extended anatomy from abdominal aorta 
to the pedal arteries. The issue with modern CT scanners is not sufficient 
speed to cover the anatomy during the arterial phase but the fact that 
scanners are so fast that they can outstrip the bolus. Another limitation 
specific to CTA is that diseased vessels are frequently calcified and it 
can be difficult to differentiate heavy calcification from intravascular 
CM, which can result in both underestimation and overestimation of 
the degree of stenosis in focal lesions and also both false-positive and 
false-negative results for segment patency. This issue can be addressed 
by postprocessing techniques that separate mural calcium and iodine 
contrast (multiplanar reformats, volume-rendering and surface-shared 
displays), by performing mask subtraction (little used in practice owing 
to technical challenges and increased radiation dose) and by using DECT 
(see earlier under CT technique). Although radiation dose has in the 
past been cited as an important factor in peripheral CTA, the CT dose 
has been substantially reduced in recent years and, particularly in the 
older cohort of patients presenting with peripheral vascular disease, is 
less important.
MAGNETIC RESONANCE ANGIOGRAPHY
Unlike CTA, there are many MR techniques that can be used to image 
the peripheral arteries. The most effective technique is contrast-enhanced 
MRA, which following a similar philosophy to CTA in that the vessels 
are imaged during first pass of a (gadolinium-based) contrast agent 
delivered intravenously. Because the maximum practically usable 
field of view is approximately 40 cm for MRA, imaging of the entire 
peripheral vasculature requires a moving-table approach with imaging 
of three successive overlapping fields of view centred over the aortoiliac, 
femoropopliteal and infrageniculate vessels in rapid succession. For 
MRA, subtraction is advisable because the high signal of marrow and 
subcutaneous fat can mask patent segments and result in overestimation 
of the degree and length of stenosis on maximum intensity projection 
(MIP) images. Another method uses Dixon-type fat suppression to 
eliminate fat signal, thus eliminating the need for a precontrast mask. 
Another advantage of contrast MRA is that multiphase imaging can be 
performed, which is highly useful for asymmetrical flow and particularly 
for visualising patency of vessels downstream to a flow-limiting lesion
