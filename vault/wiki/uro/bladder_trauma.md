# Bladder Trauma

**Domain:** [[emergency/_index|Emergency Radiology]] ([Emergency Radiology](_index.md))  
**Source:** Emergency Radiology: The Requisites (Pages 113–114)  

## Summary & Core Concepts
Bowel wall laceration involving the jejunum. Duodenal laceration after a motor vehicle accident. A duodenal injury was suspected based on the CTfindings and a focal laceration was confirmed at the time of surgery.

## Key Radiological & Clinical Findings
- **Radiography (Plain Film):** Essential baseline imaging for Bladder Trauma; evaluates anatomical contours, alignment, lucencies, and gross calcifications.
- **Computed Tomography (CT):** CT findings suggestive of focal bowel wall injury. nal trauma: Utility of 5-minute delayed CT with a reduced radiation dose.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** High soft-tissue resolution with exquisite contrast resolution for soft tissues, marrow space, and fluid kinetics.
- **Ultrasound / Doppler:** Real-time, non-ionizing dynamic assessment with color and spectral Doppler evaluation of vascular flow.

## Differential Diagnosis & High-Yield Pitfalls
- Diagnostic mimics sharing radiographic or cross-sectional morphology with Bladder Trauma.
- Key discriminator: Contrast enhancement patterns, tissue composition (fat, calcium, blood products), and anatomical distribution.
- Clinical correlation: Integration with patient presentation, laboratory biomarkers, and acuity dictates definitive diagnostic pathway.

FREE PERITONEAL FLUID
Previous studies have shown that isolated freefluid is pres­
ent in approximately 3% of all trauma CT scans. In the past,
trauma surgeons often elected to surgically explore this sub­
set of patients to fully evaluate the bowel for the presence
of injury. However, these patients are now increasingly be­
ing managed nonoperatively. One study found that only
27% of patients with isolated freefluid require therapeutic
laparotomy. Often, such patients are managed expectantly
unless other clinical criteria are present to suggest acute
bowel injury. The appearance and location of freefluid
are often helpful in selecting patients for nonoperative
management. Isolated free pelvicfluid in females is often
of no clinical significance; there is also growing evidence
to suggest that small amounts of low-attenuationfluid
in male patients may be a benignfinding, not associated
with ­ significant bowel or mesenteric injury (Fig. 3-37).
