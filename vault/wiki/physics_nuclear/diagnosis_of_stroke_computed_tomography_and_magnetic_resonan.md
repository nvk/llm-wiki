DIAGNOSIS OF STROKE: COMPUTED 
TOMOGRAPHY AND MAGNETIC RESONANCE
Outcomes for patients presenting with acute stroke have significantly 
improved over the past decade, and best functional outcomes are observed 
in patients who present early (in whom treatment can be initiated within 
6 hours of symptom onset) (Fig. 77.10). Diagnostic algorithms for 
stroke have evolved from noncontrast CT, which rarely allows affirmative 
diagnosis of infarction but which excludes haemorrhage, thus allowing 
the clinician to proceed with anticoagulation without undue risk, to 
contrast-enhanced CT techniques such as dynamic perfusion and CTA. 
Although CTA also cannot diagnose tissue infarction, it is highly sensitive 
to the presence of intravascular emboli, which are the usual cause of 
infarction. CT perfusion demonstrates reduced tissue perfusion resulting 
from infarction (again without demonstrating infarction per se) and 
multiphase imaging also allows visualisation of the collateralisation 
that accompanies infarction and assessment of the at-risk penumbra. 
CTA/perfusion is useful for the supratentorial circulation only and is 
not reliable in posterior fossa or deep nuclei infarction.
MRI offers clear superiority for diagnosis of brain infarction, can 
differentiate acute from old infarcts and has equal sensitivity for infarcts 
regardless of location. However, limited availability, particularly in an 
out-of-hours setting, longer scan times, longer patient preparation 
including delays related to confirmation of safety of implanted devices 
and challenges in monitoring patients during scanning have meant 
that MRI is not used as the primary diagnostic test in most cases of 
suspected acute stroke. This is unfortunate because diffusion-weighted 
imaging (DWI) is exquisitely sensitive to acute parenchymal infarction 
including tiny deep infarcts, and a combination of techniques including 
gradient-echo T2, diffusion and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery 
(FLAIR) imaging are highly sensitive for detecting even tiny foci of 
haemorrhage. Although traditionally the time-sensitive requirement 
to initiate treatment precluded MRI in many instances, timelines 
are becoming less important and several studies have reported that 
careful patient selection and identification of mismatch between the 
core (established infarction and therefore irrecoverable) and ischaemic 
penumbra (at threat of infarction but potentially reversible) can extend 
the timeline for thrombectomy up to 24 hours, which allows MR to 
be performed. This is of benefit because many patients will not reach 
a stroke centre within 6 hours and a large number of wake-up strokes 
(strokes noticed by patients upon wakening and time onset unknown) 
who cannot be considered for active treatment with a 6-hour window 
can now avail of thrombolysis.
Best imaging predictors of clinical outcome are size of infarct on 
CT perfusion and extent of collaterals on multiphase CTA. Diffusion 
weighted imaging and flair mismatches are a useful tool to identify 
patients eligible for thrombolysis or thrombectomy with wake-up strokes. 
Findings predicting likely recanalisation include hyperdense clot on 
CT, multisegment clot sign and M1 location within the middle cerebral 
artery. Patients with normal CTA either do not benefit or benefit less

SUMMARY BOX: CT and MR in Stroke
•	 A combination of noncontrast CT, CTA and CT perfusion allows rapid exclusion 
of haemorrhage, confirmation of vessel occlusion and assessment of the 
penumbra.
•	 MRI has a role in the selection of patients with uncertain stroke timelines 
who may still benefit from therapeutic intervention and can differentiate 
acute from old infarcts. It is also highly accurate for tiny foci of infarction 
and within the posterior fossa where CT is extremely limited.
•	 The extent of collaterals on CTA and infarct size on CT perfusion are the 
best imaging predictors of clinical outcome.
•	 MRA and CTA are both of use in evaluating the carotid and vertebral arteries 
for potential sources of emboli, but MRA is superior at identifying acute 
dissection and is not negatively impacted by plaque calcification, which 
can be a confounding feature on CTA.
