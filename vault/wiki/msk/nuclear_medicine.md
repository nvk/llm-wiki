Diffusion-Weighted Imaging
Diffusion-weighted imaging is used to identify areas of restricted diffusion 
of water molecules. Where there is no restriction of movement of water 
molecules, such as in synovial fluid, T2* signal rapidly decays. In tissues 
where the movement of water is relatively restricted, such as in tumours, 
the decay of the T2* is delayed, and signal is generated that can differenti-
ate it, using apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps, from neighbour-
ing tissues. DWI is not yet used routinely in the musculoskeletal system, 
except in myeloma staging, but there is a growing body of evidence 
that suggests it may be useful in the future for monitoring the response 
to treatment of bone tumours.
Whole-Body Magnetic Resonance Imaging
Whole-body MR imaging is now the recommended first-line imaging 
investigation for patients with suspected myeloma. It is also advocated 
for staging of children and/or adolescents with chronic recurrent 
multifocal osteomyelitis (CRMO). Protocols typically acquire T1, T2 
fat-saturated and DWI and multiple stations that are then stitched 
together into whole-body images (Fig. 38.10).
Cartilage Mapping
Cartilage mapping techniques have been the focus of considerable 
research interest over the last two decades. Automated and semi-
automated cartilage thickness and volume maps can be generated using 
a number of approaches, including finite element analysis. Physiological 
maps of cartilage composition can be generated using T2 relaxation to 
measure water content, T1 rho and delayed gadolinium-enhanced MRI 
of cartilage (dGEMRIC) to measure proteoglycan depletion. While a 
number of these techniques have been used to follow-up volunteers in 
large cohort studies, they are not routinely used in clinical practice. The 
information provided about the state of the cartilage is not yet useful 
for an individual patient at a single point in time, but indications are 
emerging such as in the monitoring of femoroacetabular impingement, 
where progressive changes in cartilage composition may trigger surgical 
intervention (Fig. 38.11).
Elastography
MR elastography can be used to image and measure the stiffness of 
musculoskeletal soft tissues. This is performed by applying a vibrating 
transducer to the skin over the area of interest and inducing a mechanical 
shear wave. The frequency of the readout gradient is tuned to the 
frequency of the transducer in order to produce an image based on the 
amplitude of the propagated shear wave. This technique has been applied 
to muscle and tendons in research settings (Fig. 38.12).
NUCLEAR MEDICINE
The radionuclide bone scintigram is the most commonly performed 
nuclear medicine imaging study in the musculoskeletal system. The 
radionuclide technetium (99mTc)-methylene diphosphonate (MDP) is 
administered as an intravenous injection and images are acquired with 
a planar gamma camera. Early image acquisition, 5 minutes after injection, 
produces blood pool images that are useful for demonstrating hyperaemia. 
Thereafter the 99mTc-MDP binds to hydroxyapatite crystals in bone. 
The rate of 99mTc-MDP binding is proportionate to the volume of local 
blood flow and the activity of osteoblasts. Areas of increased radiotracer 
uptake indicate an increase in bone turnover and, more specifically,

ARTHROGRAPHY
Conventional arthrography with fluoroscopic-guided joint injections 
of iodinated contrast material has mostly been replaced by MRI, or by 
MR or CT arthrography. Conventional arthrograms are still useful in 
two clinical settings: hydrodilatation of the shoulder and preoperative 
injections of the hind- and mid-foot.
Hydrodilatation of the glenohumeral joint can be an effective 
treatment in up to two-thirds of patients with adhesive capsulitis.

Ankle fractures
Weber B (Lauge–Hansen supination–external rotation) ankle fracture:
Oblique (left image) and lateral ankle radiographs show an oblique fracture (red arrows) through the distal 
fibula at the level of the syndesmosis (blue star). There is widening of the medial clear space (yellow arrow).
•	 Ankle sprains and fractures are the most common of all joint injuries. 
•	 The Danis–Weber classification is the simplest classification of ankle fractures and divides 
ankle fractures based on the level of the fibular fracture and resultant syndesmotic injury.
The syndesmosis is a fibrous ligamentous complex that connects the distal tibia and fibula, where 
the distal fibula fits into a groove in the distal tibia. It is formed by the anterior and posterior 
tibiofibular ligaments. The interosseous membrane connects the lengths of the tibia and fibula, 
superior to the syndesmosis.
•	 Danis–Weber A is a distal fibular fracture with an intact syndesmosis.
•	 Danis–Weber B is a more proximal transsyndesmotic fracture, usually associated with 
partial syndesmotic rupture.
•	 Danis–Weber C is a high fibular fracture above the level of the syndesmosis, usually 
associated with total syndesmotic rupture and subsequent ankle mortise instability.
•	 The Lauge–Hansen classification divides ankle fractures based on the directional 
mechanism of trauma. A supination–adduction injury correlates to Weber A; 
supination–external rotation correlates to Weber B; and pronation–external rotation 
correlates to Weber C.
Named ankle fractures
• Tillaux fracture is a Salter Harris III fracture of the lateral tibial epiphyses, typically 
seen in adolescents.
• Triplane fracture (also known as a Marmor–Lynn fracture) represents three distinct 
fractures through the distal tibia: A vertical epiphyseal fracture, horizontal physeal 
fracture, and oblique metaphyseal fracture. 
• Wagstaffe–LeFort fracture is an avulsion of the anterior distal fibula at site of insertion 
of the anterior tibiofibular ligament.
• 
Maisonneuve fracture is a high (proximal) fibular fracture associated with an interosseous 
membrane tear or medial malleolar fracture. Isolated imaging of the ankle may only detect 
subtle widening of the ankle mortise, which should prompt a more proximal radiograph.

Meniscal cyst
•
A meniscal cyst is loculated fluid adjacent to the meniscus, thought to result from 
extension of joint fluid through a meniscal tear.
•
Meniscal cysts are most commonly associated with horizontal cleavage tears.
•
It is important to mention the presence of a meniscal cyst because the meniscal cyst 
may continue to cause symptoms even if the meniscus is resected. Not all cysts are
able to be visualized by arthroscopy.

•
Discoid meniscus is a spectrum of congenital malformations of the meniscus that
ranges from increased thickness of the meniscus to a complete disc-like shape. Discoid
meniscus is defined by having a portion of the meniscus extending to the central tibial 
plateau.
•
Discoid meniscus typically affects children and is a common cause of joint-line pain, 
clicking, or locking in a child or adolescent. Discoid meniscus is prone to undergo
cystic degeneration and tear. Even in the absence of a tear, a discoid meniscus may be
symptomatic.
•
The lateral meniscus is more commonly affected.
•
On MRI, a meniscus is discoid if an oval or bow tie-shaped meniscus is seen on 
three or more contiguous sagittal slices with standard 4 mm-thick sagittal slices 
(representing 12 mm in medial–lateral thickness).
