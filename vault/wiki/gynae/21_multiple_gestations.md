Ultrasound plays an important role in the assessment of 
women carrying multiple gestations. Sonography is used to 
evaluate the number of gestations; characterize the type of 
twinning; assess fetal anatomy, growth, and complications; 
and guide diagnostic and therapeutic interventions. As a 
group, multiple gestation pregnancies are at increased risk for 
complications (e.g., growth restriction, preterm delivery, vela-
mentous umbilical cord insertion, and congenital anomalies) 
compared with singleton pregnancies. Additional complica-
tions unique to multiple gestations such as twin-to-twin 
transfusion and umbilical cord entanglement affect subsets of 
multigestational pregnancies depending on their chorionicity 
and amnionicity. Although the main focus of this chapter is 
on twin pregnancies, many of the concepts underlying ultra-
sound evaluation of twins are also applicable to higher-order 
multigestation pregnancies.
TYPES OF MULTIPLE GESTATIONS
Monozygotic versus Dizygotic Pregnancies
Twin pregnancies are categorized into monozygotic and dizy-
gotic gestations. Monozygotic twinning occurs when a single 
egg is fertilized by a sperm and the conceptus subsequently 
divides into two embryos. Monozygotic twins are widely 
referred to as identical twins because the twins have the 
same genetic composition and gender and are usually similar 
in appearance. Amnionicity and chorionicity of monozygotic 
twins are determined by the timing of cleavage into two 
embryos as follows: day 0 to 3, dichorionic diamniotic; day 4 
to 8, monochorionic diamniotic; day 9 to 12, monochorionic 
monoamniotic; and day 13 to 15, conjoined. Approximately 
one third of monozygotic twins are dichorionic diamniotic. 
Most of the remaining monozygotic twins are monochori-
onic diamniotic. Monochorionic monoamniotic twinning is 
rare, accounting for less than 1% of monozygotic twin 
pregnancies.
Dizygotic twinning occurs when two ova are each fertilized 
by a different sperm. Dizygotic twins are frequently referred 
to as fraternal twins. They are of dissimilar genetic makeup 
and typically are phenotypically different after birth. Dizygotic 
twins are virtually always dichorionic diamniotic. Dizygotic 
twins can be the same or discordant in gender. Multiple 
factors inﬂuence the likelihood of dizygotic twinning includ-
ing maternal age, geographic area, race, and assisted repro-
ductive interventions. The incidence of multiple gestations 
has increased substantially in recent years due to assisted 
reproductive procedures that dramatically increase the 
number of dizygotic and, to a lesser extent, monozygotic 
pregnancies.
Chorionicity and Amnionicity: General Concepts
The chorionicity and amnionicity of a multigestation preg-
nancy inﬂuence the likelihood of complications such as twin-
to-twin transfusion and the availability and types of therapeutic 
options. Ultrasound plays a critical role in assessing chorion-
icity and amnionicity.
Dichorionic diamniotic twinning is characterized by two 
gestational sacs, each containing one twin. Both sacs contain 
a layer of amnion centrally and a layer of chorion peripher-
ally. Thus the membrane between the twins is thick, consist-
ing of a total of four layers: a layer of amnion and a layer of 
chorion from the gestational sac of each twin (Fig. 21-1A). In 
monochorionic diamniotic twinning each twin is also sur-
rounded by a separate gestational sac, but the portion of the 
sac between the twins only contains amnion. The twins share 
a common chorion that surrounds the two gestational sacs. 
The membrane in a monochorionic diamniotic pregnancy is 
thinner than in a dichorionic diamniotic pregnancy because 
the amnion is thinner than the chorion and the membrane 
between monochorionic diamniotic twins consists of two 
layers of amnion, one from each gestational sac, and no layers 
of chorion (see Fig. 21-1B). There is no such thing as dicho-
rionic monoamniotic twinning because the chorion is periph-
eral to the amnion. Monochorionic monoamniotic twinning 
occurs when the twins occupy a single gestational sac consist-
ing of the amnion centrally and the chorion peripherally with 
no intertwin membrane (see Fig. 21-1C). Conjoined twinning 
describes twins that fail to completely separate from each 
other and is the rarest form of monochorionic monoamniotic 
twinning.
Dichorionic diamniotic twinning has the lowest incidence 
of complications. Monochorionic diamniotic twins are at risk 
for twin-to-twin transfusion and acardiac twinning due to 
vascular anastomoses in the placenta. Monochorionic mono-
amniotic twins are at risk for umbilical cord entanglement 
because of the absence of an intertwin membrane.
Chorionicity and Amnionicity:  
First-Trimester Assessment
The ﬁrst trimester is the best time in pregnancy to establish 
chorionicity and amnionicity with sonography. Ultrasound 
ﬁndings helpful in making this determination include the 
number of gestational sacs, number of yolk sacs, number of 
placental sites, membrane thickness, and twin peak sign.
Diamniotic twinning can be conﬁdently diagnosed when 
two gestational sacs are visualized (Fig. 21-2A). When two

when one yolk sac is seen, it is likely monoamniotic (see Fig.

distinguishing between diamniotic and monoamniotic preg-

yolk sac with nonvisualization of an intertwin membrane 
usually indicates a monochorionic monoamniotic pregnancy 
(see Fig 21-2E and F). In a minority of monochorionic diam-
niotic pregnancies, however, only a single yolk sac is initially 
seen. Follow-up ultrasound is indicated to conﬁrm absence of 
a second yolk sac and absence of an intertwin membrane 
before deﬁnitively diagnosing monochorionic monoamniotic 
twinning based on yolk sac number and gestational sac 
number.
Dichorionic pregnancies can be diagnosed by identifying a 
thick dividing septum between the twins (Fig. 21-3A and B). 
Membrane thickness is more reliable in assessing chorionicity 
during the ﬁrst trimester than during the second and third 
trimesters because differences in membrane thickness 
between monochorionic and dichorionic twins tend to be less 
pronounced later in pregnancy. Identiﬁcation of two separate 
placentas is diagnostic of dichorionic diamniotic twinning 
and is occasionally seen near the end of the ﬁrst trimester 
(see Fig. 21-3C). Another sign of dichorionic diamniotic twin-
ning is the twin peak sign, which refers to a triangular projec-
tion of placental tissue extending between the layers of the 
attachment of the intertwin membrane to the placenta (see 
Fig. 21-3D).
