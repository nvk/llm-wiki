# Esophageal Atresia

## Key Radiological & Clinical Findings
- **Radiography (Plain Film):** Essential baseline imaging for Esophageal Atresia; evaluates anatomical contours, alignment, lucencies, and gross calcifications.
- **Computed Tomography (CT):** Gold-standard modality for multiplanar delineation, density characterization, and staging of Esophageal Atresia.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** High soft-tissue resolution with exquisite contrast resolution for soft tissues, marrow space, and fluid kinetics.
- **Ultrasound / Doppler:** Real-time, non-ionizing dynamic assessment with color and spectral Doppler evaluation of vascular flow.

## Differential Diagnosis & High-Yield Pitfalls
- Diagnostic mimics sharing radiographic or cross-sectional morphology with Esophageal Atresia.
- Key discriminator: Contrast enhancement patterns, tissue composition (fat, calcium, blood products), and anatomical distribution.
- Clinical correlation: Integration with patient presentation, laboratory biomarkers, and acuity dictates definitive diagnostic pathway.

Esophageal Atresia
Congenital atresia of the esophagus is a rare abnormality
that may occur as an isolated abnormality or as part of a
larger spectrum of abnormalities. In most cases there is an as­
sociatedfistula to the trachea. The diagnosis is almost always
suspected clinically when an infant has trouble feeding, or
there is difficulty passing a nasogastric tube into the s­tomach.
Radiographs help to confirm the diagnosis when there is no
gas identified within any bowel loops in the abdomen. If
placement of a nasogastric tube has been attempted, the tube
will be identified above the diaphragm on the radiograph, of­
ten in a dilated, gas-filled, esophageal pouch (Fig. 6-13).
