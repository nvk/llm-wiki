# Neonatal Pneumonia

**Domain:** [[emergency/_index|Emergency Radiology]] ([Emergency Radiology](_index.md))  
**Source:** Emergency Radiology: The Requisites (Pages 200–201)  

## Summary & Core Concepts
Group B streptococcus.

## Key Radiological & Clinical Findings
- **Radiography (Plain Film):** Essential baseline imaging for Neonatal Pneumonia; evaluates anatomical contours, alignment, lucencies, and gross calcifications.
- **Computed Tomography (CT):** Gold-standard modality for multiplanar delineation, density characterization, and staging of Neonatal Pneumonia.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** High soft-tissue resolution with exquisite contrast resolution for soft tissues, marrow space, and fluid kinetics.
- **Ultrasound / Doppler:** Real-time, non-ionizing dynamic assessment with color and spectral Doppler evaluation of vascular flow.

## Differential Diagnosis & High-Yield Pitfalls
- Diagnostic mimics sharing radiographic or cross-sectional morphology with Neonatal Pneumonia.
- Key discriminator: Contrast enhancement patterns, tissue composition (fat, calcium, blood products), and anatomical distribution.
- Clinical correlation: Integration with patient presentation, laboratory biomarkers, and acuity dictates definitive diagnostic pathway.

Meconium Aspiration Syndrome
MAS is diagnosed by the presence of meconium below
the level of the ­ vocal cords at birth. Infants with MAS
are usually post-­mature, or have experienced intrauterine
stress. The thick, tenacious meconium causes obstruction
of small- and medium-sized airways, which leads to areas
of both atelectasis and overinflation. The meconium can
cause a chemical pneumonitis and also inactivates surfac­
tant within lung alveoli. Chest radiographs in infants with
MAS reveal coarsened interstitial densities and bilateral
parenchymal opacities interspersed with areas of hyper­
aeration (Fig. 6-7). MAS is the most common respiratory
disease to cause a pleural effusion in thefirst few days of
life.
Posterior Urethral Valves
Posterior urethral valves (PUVs) are the most common
congenital cause of bilateral renal obstruction. Their em­
bryologic development is complex and has been previously
described in the literature. Briefly, PUVs consist of an ob­
structing membrane, or persistent urogenital membrane,
at the level of the verumontanum in the posterior urethra
of male infants. This membrane causes varying degrees of
bladder outlet obstruction and bilateral hydroureterone­
phrosis. The diagnosis is often made in utero and should
be suspected in any male patient who fails to void within
24 hours of birth.
