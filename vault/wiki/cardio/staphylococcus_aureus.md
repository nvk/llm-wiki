# Staphylococcus aureus

**Domain:** [[cardio/_index|Cardiothoracic Radiology]] ([Cardiothoracic Radiology](_index.md))  
**Source:** Thoracic Radiology: The Requisites (Pages 100–101)  

## Summary & Core Concepts
Parapneumonic effusion (pneumococcal pneumonia). A, The posteroanterior view shows a right upper lobe consolidation.

## Key Radiological & Clinical Findings
- **Radiography (Plain Film):** frequent radiographic presentation.
- **Computed Tomography (CT):** Gold-standard modality for multiplanar delineation, density characterization, and staging of Staphylococcus aureus.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** High soft-tissue resolution with exquisite contrast resolution for soft tissues, marrow space, and fluid kinetics.
- **Ultrasound / Doppler:** Real-time, non-ionizing dynamic assessment with color and spectral Doppler evaluation of vascular flow.

## Differential Diagnosis & High-Yield Pitfalls
- Diagnostic mimics sharing radiographic or cross-sectional morphology with Staphylococcus aureus.
- Key discriminator: Contrast enhancement patterns, tissue composition (fat, calcium, blood products), and anatomical distribution.
- Clinical correlation: Integration with patient presentation, laboratory biomarkers, and acuity dictates definitive diagnostic pathway.

Staphylococcus aureus
S. aureus (Box 3-3) is a gram-positive coccus, and the spher­
ical organisms occur in pairs and clusters. This pneumo­
nia rarely develops in healthy adults, but it is sometimes
a complication of viral infections and is much more com­
mon in infants and children. In infants, unilateral or bilat­
eral consolidation involving the lower lungs is the most
frequent radiographic presentation. Pneumatoceles, thin-
walled cystsfilled with air or partiallyfilled withfluid, may
develop and occasionally rupture into the pleural space,
resulting in pneumothorax. In adults, the disease is usually
bilateral and is preceded by an atypical pneumonia such as
influenza. Cavitation is a common feature, and the cavities
may be multiple, thick walled, and irregular (Fig. 3-13).
There is a high incidence of large pleural effusions, and
empyema resulting from bronchopleuralfistula is a com­
mon complication. Methicillin resistant staphylococcus
aureus (MRSA)  pneumonia usually occurs as a nosocomial
infection in health care centers particularly in older, immu­
nocompromised  or intensive care unit patients.
Staphylococcal infection in the lungs may occur by way
of the hematogenous route. This is usually the result of sep­
tic emboli, which arise in the central veins or as ­vegetations
on cardiac valves, particularly in intravenous drug abusers
and patients with indwelling ­intravenous ­catheters. The
radiographic appearance is that of multiple nodular masses
with or without cavitation, as previously described.
Streptococcus pyogenes
Streptococci (Box 3-4) are gram-positive cocci that occur
in pairs and chains. The pneumonia occasionally occurs in
epidemic proportions. This form of pneumonia is much
less common than that caused by Staphylococcus or S. pneu­
moniae (pneumococcus).
The radiographic features include lower lobe consolidation,
often occurring with a segmental distribution. Pleural effu­
sions occur frequently, but localized empyema is unusual.
