The standard second and third trimester fetal ultrasound 
examination includes evaluation of the kidneys, urinary 
bladder, and amniotic ﬂuid volume. A signiﬁcant abnormality 
of both kidneys or of the urinary bladder can result in oligo-
hydramnios. Cyclical changes in the size of the urinary 
bladder suggest that at least one kidney is producing urine. 
The normal fetal ureters are not typically identiﬁed, although 
when dilated, the ureters are more readily seen.
NORMAL FETAL KIDNEYS AND BLADDER
Optimal assessment of the fetal kidneys includes evaluation 
of images of the kidneys in the axial and longitudinal scan 
planes. Ultrasound depicts the fetal kidneys as ovoid struc-
tures on either side of the spine in the axial scan plane. Early 
in the second trimester the kidneys are isoechoic to slightly 
hyperechoic to the adjacent abdominal contents, and it may 
be difﬁcult to distinguish them from the surrounding tissues. 
Identiﬁcation of a small amount of ﬂuid in the renal pelvis is 
helpful in conﬁrming renal visualization (Fig. 18-1A). In the 
later stages of pregnancy, the margins of the kidneys are 
easier to perceive because they are delineated by interfaces 
from the renal capsule and perinephric fat (see Fig. 18-1B). 
Corticomedullary differentiation becomes progressively more 
apparent during the third trimester. The medullary pyramids 
are depicted as discrete hypoechoic structures that are less 
echogenic than the renal cortex (see Fig. 18-1C). The medul-
lary pyramids should not be mistaken for dilated calyces. The 
renal arteries can be seen extending from the aorta toward 
the kidneys on color Doppler (see Fig. 18-1D).
Renal size can be assessed subjectively. Nomograms of 
renal size are available for reference when subjective evalua-
tion suggests abnormal size. The kidneys increase in size with 
advancing gestation. As a general guideline, the normal fetal 
kidneys appear to occupy less than one third of the abdomen 
in axial projection. At term the fetal kidney should measure 
less than 5.5 cm in length.
The kidneys start to produce urine at approximately 10 
weeks of gestation. Fetal urination accounts for the majority 
of the amniotic ﬂuid by 16 weeks. The urinary bladder is 
visualized as a ﬂuid-ﬁlled structure in the midline of the pelvis 
(Fig. 18-2A and B). It is often seen in the late ﬁrst trimester 
and should be routinely visualized by 14 to 16 weeks of gesta-
tion. Axial imaging with color Doppler is helpful in conﬁrming 
bladder visualization, showing the intra-abdominal exten-
sions of the umbilical arteries along the lateral margins of the 
bladder (see Fig. 18-2C). The bladder ﬁlls and empties in a 
cyclical fashion, corresponding to distention of the bladder 
by urine produced by the kidneys followed by fetal urination 
into the amniotic cavity. Observation of changes in bladder 
size during the course of an ultrasound examination is helpful 
in distinguishing the bladder from other cystic structures in 
the pelvis (Fig. 18-3).
ABNORMAL KIDNEY LOCATION AND NUMBER
When ultrasound fails to identify the kidney in its expected 
position in the renal fossa, an abnormally located kidney, 
small kidney, or absent kidney should be suspected (Fig. 
18-4A). The adrenal gland often extends into the renal fossa 
in the absence of a kidney in the fossa, assuming a ﬂattened 
elongated conﬁguration termed the lying down adrenal sign 
in longitudinal scan planes (see Fig. 18-4B). The lying down 
adrenal is distinguished from the kidney by its ﬂattened con-
ﬁguration, with a linear, echogenic center and a hypoechoic 
periphery. The lying down adrenal should not be mistaken for 
a kidney, which has a more ovoid reniform shape in longitu-
dinal scan planes.
The pelvis and abdomen should be carefully scanned to 
assess for an ectopic kidney when the kidney is not identiﬁed 
in the renal fossa. The most common abnormality of renal 
location is the pelvic kidney. A pelvic kidney can be difﬁcult 
to visualize, as it may be small, malrotated, or similar in 
echogenicity to the surrounding structures. Pelvic kidneys are 
frequently found adjacent to the urinary bladder (see Fig. 
18-4C). Visualization of medullary pyramids or the blood 
vessels supplying the kidney may also be helpful in recogniz-
ing a pelvic kidney (see Fig. 18-4D to F). Pelvic kidneys are 
at an increased risk of obstruction. When a kidney is not 
identiﬁed in its expected location in the ﬂank, identiﬁcation 
of a circumscribed ﬂuid collection in the fetal pelvis should 
raise suspicion of a pelvic kidney with a dilated collecting 
system (Fig. 18-5). Other less commonly identiﬁed abnormal 
fetal kidney conﬁgurations include horseshoe kidney and 
crossed fused ectopia, in which the ectopic kidney is fused to 
the lower pole of the contralateral kidney (Fig. 18-6).
Unilateral renal agenesis is more common than bilateral 
agenesis. Ultrasound ﬁndings of isolated unilateral renal 
agenesis are nonvisualization of a kidney and a lying down 
adrenal conﬁguration on the involved side (Fig. 18-7A to C). 
Compensatory hypertrophy of the contralateral kidney is 
occasionally seen. Color Doppler shows absence of the cor-
responding renal artery (see Fig. 18-7D). The bladder and 
amniotic ﬂuid volume should be normal in isolated unilateral 
renal agenesis due to the production of urine by the intact 
kidney. It is important to assess the contralateral kidney care-
fully because there is an increased likelihood of contralateral 
renal abnormalities (e.g., vesicoureteral reﬂux and uretero-
pelvic junction [UPJ] obstruction).
